🎓 Nền Tảng Toán Học - Zero-Knowledge Proof Systems

Zero-Knowledge Proofs (ZKP) là các giao thức mật mã học cho phép một bên (người chứng minh - Prover \(\mathcal{P}\)) chứng minh với bên kia (người xác minh - Verifier \(\mathcal{V}\)) rằng một mệnh đề là đúng, mà KHÔNG tiết lộ bất kỳ thông tin nào ngoài sự đúng đắn của mệnh đề đó.

🧮 Nền Tảng Lý Thuyết Độ Phức Tạp (Complexity Theory)

Zero-knowledge proofs có mối liên hệ chặt chẽ với lý thuyết độ phức tạp tính toán. Chúng ta làm việc với quan hệ NP (NP relations):

Định nghĩa Formal: Một quan hệ nhị phân \(R \subseteq \{0,1\}^* \times \{0,1\}^*\) thuộc NP nếu tồn tại thuật toán thời gian đa thức \(V_R\) sao cho:

\[(x, w) \in R \Leftrightarrow V_R(x, w) = 1 \text{ và } |w| \leq \text{poly}(|x|)\]

Trong đó:

  • \(x\) là tuyên bố (statement) - điều cần chứng minh
  • \(w\) là chứng cứ (witness) - bí mật mà prover biết
  • Ngôn ngữ \(L_R = \{x : \exists w, (x,w) \in R\}\)

Ví dụ Cụ Thể: Graph 3-Coloring (Tô màu đồ thị)

• Tuyên bố \(x\): Đồ thị \(G = (V, E)\) có thể tô được bằng 3 màu

• Chứng cứ \(w\): Một cách tô màu hợp lệ \(c: V \rightarrow \{Đỏ, Xanh lá, Xanh dương\}\)

• Quan hệ: \((G, c) \in R\) nếu với mọi cạnh \((u,v) \in E\): \(c(u) \neq c(v)\)

• Tính chất: Bài toán NP-complete - không có thuật toán nhanh được biết

Formal Definition of Zero-Knowledge Proof System

A zero-knowledge proof system for a language \(L \subseteq \{0,1\}^*\) is a two-party protocol between prover \(\mathcal{P}\) and verifier \(\mathcal{V}\) that satisfies three fundamental properties:

🧮 The Three Pillars of Zero-Knowledge

1. Completeness (Correctness)

If the statement is true and both parties follow the protocol, the verifier will be convinced:

\[\forall x \in L: \Pr[\langle \mathcal{P}(w), \mathcal{V} \rangle(x) = \text{accept}] \geq 1 - \text{negl}(\lambda)\]

where \(w\) is the witness (secret knowledge), \(\lambda\) is the security parameter, and negl denotes a negligible function.

2. Soundness (Security)

If the statement is false, no cheating prover can convince the verifier, except with negligible probability:

\[\forall x \notin L, \forall \mathcal{P}^*: \Pr[\langle \mathcal{P}^*, \mathcal{V} \rangle(x) = \text{accept}] \leq \text{negl}(\lambda)\]

For interactive proofs, we often have computational soundness (argument systems) where the bound holds against polynomial-time adversaries.

3. Zero-Knowledge Property

The interaction reveals nothing beyond the truth of the statement. Formally, there exists a simulator \(\mathcal{S}\) such that for all \(x \in L\):

\[\{\text{View}_{\mathcal{V}^*}[\langle \mathcal{P}(w), \mathcal{V}^* \rangle(x)]\} \approx \{\mathcal{S}(x)\}\]

where \(\text{View}_{\mathcal{V}^*}\) is the view of any verifier \(\mathcal{V}^*\) (including messages and random coins), and \(\approx\) denotes computational indistinguishability.

📐 Schnorr's Protocol: A Complete Mathematical Treatment

Setup and Notation

Let \(p\) be a large prime, \(q\) a prime divisor of \(p-1\), and \(g\) a generator of order \(q\) in \(\mathbb{Z}_p^*\). The discrete logarithm problem: given \(y = g^x \bmod p\), find \(x\).

Protocol: Proving Knowledge of \(x\) where \(y = g^x \bmod p\)

Public: \(p, q, g, y\) where \(y = g^x \bmod p\)

Prover's secret: \(x \in \mathbb{Z}_q\)

Step 1 (Commitment): Prover chooses random \(r \xleftarrow{\$} \mathbb{Z}_q\) and computes:

\[t = g^r \bmod p\]

Prover sends \(t\) to Verifier.

Step 2 (Challenge): Verifier chooses random \(c \xleftarrow{\$} \mathbb{Z}_q\) and sends \(c\) to Prover.

Step 3 (Response): Prover computes:

\[s = r + cx \bmod q\]

Prover sends \(s\) to Verifier.

Step 4 (Verification): Verifier accepts if and only if:

\[g^s \equiv t \cdot y^c \pmod{p}\]

Correctness Proof

If both parties are honest, we have:

\[\begin{align} g^s &= g^{r + cx} \\ &= g^r \cdot g^{cx} \\ &= t \cdot (g^x)^c \\ &= t \cdot y^c \pmod{p} \end{align}\]

Soundness Proof (Knowledge Extraction)

Theorem: If a prover can produce two valid responses \(s_1, s_2\) for the same commitment \(t\) but different challenges \(c_1 \neq c_2\), then we can extract the discrete logarithm \(x\).

Proof: Given two valid transcripts \((t, c_1, s_1)\) and \((t, c_2, s_2)\), we have:

\[\begin{cases} g^{s_1} = t \cdot y^{c_1} \\ g^{s_2} = t \cdot y^{c_2} \end{cases}\]

Dividing these equations:

\[\begin{align} g^{s_1 - s_2} &= y^{c_1 - c_2} \\ g^{s_1 - s_2} &= (g^x)^{c_1 - c_2} \\ g^{s_1 - s_2} &= g^{x(c_1 - c_2)} \end{align}\]

Therefore, in \(\mathbb{Z}_q\):

\[x = \frac{s_1 - s_2}{c_1 - c_2} \bmod q\]

This extraction procedure shows that any prover who succeeds with non-negligible probability must "know" \(x\). □

Zero-Knowledge Property (Honest-Verifier ZK)

Theorem: The Schnorr protocol is honest-verifier zero-knowledge.

Proof: We construct a simulator \(\mathcal{S}\) that produces indistinguishable transcripts without knowing \(x\):

Simulator \(\mathcal{S}(p, q, g, y)\):

1. Choose random \(c, s \xleftarrow{\$} \mathbb{Z}_q\)

2. Compute \(t = g^s \cdot y^{-c} \bmod p\)

3. Output transcript \((t, c, s)\)

The simulated transcript \((t, c, s)\) satisfies the verification equation by construction:

\[g^s = g^s \cdot y^0 = g^s \cdot y^{c} \cdot y^{-c} = t \cdot y^c\]

In real execution: \((t, c, s)\) where \(t = g^r\), \(c\) is random, \(s = r + cx\).

In simulation: \((t, c, s)\) where \(s, c\) are random, \(t = g^s \cdot y^{-c}\).

Both distributions are uniform over valid transcripts, hence perfectly indistinguishable. □

🔒 Commitment Schemes: Mathematical Framework

Definition

A commitment scheme is a two-phase protocol with:

Commit phase: \(c \leftarrow \text{Commit}(m; r)\) where \(m\) is message, \(r\) is randomness

Reveal phase: \(\text{Verify}(c, m, r) \rightarrow \{\text{accept}, \text{reject}\}\)

Properties

Binding: Cannot find \(m \neq m'\) with valid openings to same commitment

\[\Pr[\exists m \neq m', r, r': \text{Commit}(m;r) = \text{Commit}(m';r')] \leq \text{negl}(\lambda)\]

Hiding: Commitment reveals no information about message

\[\{\text{Commit}(m_0; r)\}_{r} \approx \{\text{Commit}(m_1; r)\}_{r}\]

Pedersen Commitment

Setup: Group \(\mathbb{G}\) of prime order \(q\), generators \(g, h\) where \(\log_g h\) is unknown.

\[\text{Com}(m; r) = g^m \cdot h^r\]

Perfect Hiding: For any \(m_0, m_1 \in \mathbb{Z}_q\), the distributions are identical:

\[\{g^{m_0} \cdot h^r : r \xleftarrow{\$} \mathbb{Z}_q\} \equiv \{g^{m_1} \cdot h^r : r \xleftarrow{\$} \mathbb{Z}_q\}\]

Computational Binding: Breaking binding requires finding \(\log_g h\) (discrete log problem).

Homomorphic Property: Pedersen commitments are additively homomorphic:

\[\text{Com}(m_1; r_1) \cdot \text{Com}(m_2; r_2) = g^{m_1+m_2} \cdot h^{r_1+r_2} = \text{Com}(m_1 + m_2; r_1 + r_2)\]

🎨 Graph 3-Coloring: Zero-Knowledge for NP-Complete Problems

Problem Statement

Given a graph \(G = (V, E)\) with \(n\) vertices and a valid 3-coloring \(c: V \rightarrow \{1, 2, 3\}\), prove that \(G\) is 3-colorable without revealing the coloring.

Significance: Graph 3-coloring is NP-complete. A ZK proof for it shows ZK proofs exist for all NP languages (by Cook-Levin theorem).

The Protocol (Goldreich-Micali-Wigderson, 1991)

Setup: Prover knows valid coloring \(c: V \rightarrow \{1,2,3\}\). Public: graph \(G = (V,E)\).

Round Protocol (repeat many times):

1. Commitment Phase:

• Prover chooses random permutation \(\pi: \{1,2,3\} \rightarrow \{1,2,3\}\)

• For each vertex \(v \in V\), commit to permuted color:

\(C_v = \text{Commit}(\pi(c(v)); r_v)\)

• Send all commitments \(\{C_v\}_{v \in V}\) to verifier

2. Challenge Phase:

• Verifier randomly selects an edge \(e = (u, v) \in E\)

• Send \(e\) to prover

3. Response Phase:

• Prover reveals \(\pi(c(u)), \pi(c(v))\) and randomness \(r_u, r_v\)

4. Verification:

• Verify commitments: \(C_u = \text{Commit}(\pi(c(u)); r_u)\) and \(C_v = \text{Commit}(\pi(c(v)); r_v)\)

• Check colors differ: \(\pi(c(u)) \neq \pi(c(v))\)

Proof of Properties

Completeness: If prover has valid coloring and follows protocol, adjacent vertices have different colors, so verification always passes. □

Soundness:

Theorem: If graph is not 3-colorable, cheating prover succeeds in single round with probability at most \(\frac{|E|-1}{|E|}\).

Proof: Without valid coloring, there exists at least one edge \((u,v)\) where any attempted coloring has \(c(u) = c(v)\). When verifier randomly selects this edge, prover cannot produce valid response. Since edge is chosen uniformly from \(|E|\) edges, probability of avoiding the bad edge is at most \(\frac{|E|-1}{|E|}\).

After \(k\) rounds: soundness error \(\leq \left(\frac{|E|-1}{|E|}\right)^k \approx e^{-k/|E|}\)

For \(k = 128|E|\) rounds, soundness error \(< 2^{-128}\). □

Zero-Knowledge:

Theorem: The protocol is honest-verifier zero-knowledge.

Proof (Simulator Construction):

Simulator \(\mathcal{S}(G)\): (Given only graph, no coloring)

1. Randomly select edge \(e^* = (u^*, v^*) \in E\)

2. Choose two different random colors \(\alpha, \beta \in \{1,2,3\}, \alpha \neq \beta\)

3. Set \(C_{u^*} = \text{Commit}(\alpha; r_{u^*})\), \(C_{v^*} = \text{Commit}(\beta; r_{v^*})\)

4. For all other \(w \neq u^*, v^*\): \(C_w = \text{Commit}(0; r_w)\) (dummy commitments)

5. Output transcript: \((\{C_v\}_v, e^*, \alpha, \beta, r_{u^*}, r_{v^*})\)

6. If verifier's challenge \(e \neq e^*\), rewind and try again

Expected number of attempts: \(|E|\). The simulated transcript is distributed identically to real transcripts (commitments are perfectly hiding, revealed colors are uniform and different). □

Practical Efficiency

• Communication per round: \(O(n)\) commitments + 2 openings

• Rounds needed: \(O(|E|)\) for soundness \(2^{-128}\)

• Total communication: \(O(n \cdot |E|)\)

Modern ZK-SNARKs can prove graph 3-colorability with \(O(1)\) proof size!

🌳 Merkle Trees: Mathematical Foundation

Definition and Construction

A Merkle tree is a binary tree where each node contains a cryptographic hash. For a set of data blocks \(D = \{d_1, d_2, \ldots, d_n\}\):

Leaf level: Hash each data block

\[\text{leaf}_i = H(d_i) \text{ for } i = 1, \ldots, n\]

Internal nodes: Hash concatenation of children

\[\text{node}_{i,j} = H(\text{node}_{i-1, 2j} \| \text{node}_{i-1, 2j+1})\]

Root: The hash at the top of the tree

\[\text{root} = \text{node}_{\log_2 n, 0}\]

Membership Proof

To prove \(d_k \in D\), the prover provides:

1. The data element \(d_k\)

2. Authentication path: sibling hashes \(\{\text{sib}_0, \text{sib}_1, \ldots, \text{sib}_{\log_2 n - 1}\}\)

Verification: Compute path from leaf to root:

\[\begin{align} h_0 &= H(d_k) \\ h_1 &= H(h_0 \| \text{sib}_0) \text{ or } H(\text{sib}_0 \| h_0) \\ &\vdots \\ h_{\log_2 n} &= \text{root} \end{align}\]

Security Analysis

Theorem: If \(H\) is collision-resistant, then finding a valid proof for \(d \notin D\) requires breaking collision resistance.

Proof size: \(O(\log n)\) hash values

Verification time: \(O(\log n)\) hash computations

🔬 Advanced ZK Constructions: Mathematical Deep Dive

1. Non-Interactive Zero-Knowledge (NIZK)

Transform interactive protocols to non-interactive using the Fiat-Shamir heuristic:

Replace verifier's random challenge \(c\) with hash of transcript:

\[c = H(\text{statement} \| \text{commitment})\]

Security: Secure in the Random Oracle Model (ROM), where \(H\) is modeled as truly random function.

2. zk-SNARKs (Zero-Knowledge Succinct Non-Interactive ARguments of Knowledge)

Key Properties

• Succinct: Proof size \(O(1)\), verification time \(O(1)\)

• Non-interactive: Single message from prover to verifier

• Knowledge soundness: Prover must "know" a witness

Mathematical Framework: QAP (Quadratic Arithmetic Programs)

Convert computation to polynomial constraints. For statement \(x\) and witness \(w\):

Step 1: Express computation as arithmetic circuit with gates:

\[a_i \cdot b_i = c_i \text{ for } i = 1, \ldots, m\]

Step 2: Convert to polynomials \(A(x), B(x), C(x)\) such that:

\[A(x) \cdot B(x) - C(x) = H(x) \cdot Z(x)\]

where \(Z(x) = \prod_{i=1}^m (x - r_i)\) is the target polynomial with roots at evaluation points.

Groth16 Protocol (Simplified)

Setup (Trusted): Generate common reference string (CRS) with "toxic waste" \(\tau\):

\[\text{CRS} = \{g^{\tau^i}, g^{\alpha \tau^i}, g^{\beta \tau^i}\}_{i=0}^d\]

Prove: For witness \(w\), compute polynomials and evaluate at secret \(\tau\):

\[\pi = ([A(\tau)]_1, [B(\tau)]_2, [C(\tau)]_1)\]

Verify: Pairing check:

\[e(A, B) = e(\alpha, \beta) \cdot e(C, \delta) \cdot e(\text{public}, \gamma)\]

where \(e: \mathbb{G}_1 \times \mathbb{G}_2 \rightarrow \mathbb{G}_T\) is a bilinear pairing.

Security Intuition

• Soundness: Relies on Knowledge of Exponent (KEA) assumption in bilinear groups

• Zero-knowledge: Add random blinding factors to polynomials

• Succinctness: Constant-size proof regardless of circuit size

3. zk-STARKs (Zero-Knowledge Scalable Transparent ARguments of Knowledge)

Key Advantages over SNARKs

• Transparent: No trusted setup required

• Post-quantum secure: Based on hash functions and error-correcting codes

• Scalable: Prover time \(O(n \log n)\), verifier time \(O(\log^2 n)\)

Mathematical Framework: Polynomial IOPs

Step 1: Arithmetization

Express computation as polynomial constraint system:

\[P(x, y, z) = 0 \text{ for all steps of computation}\]

Step 2: Low-Degree Extension (LDE)

Extend polynomial to larger domain and evaluate:

\[f: D \rightarrow \mathbb{F} \text{ extends to } \tilde{f}: L \rightarrow \mathbb{F} \text{ where } |L| \gg |D|\]

Step 3: FRI Protocol (Fast Reed-Solomon IOP)

Prove polynomial has low degree through recursive folding:

For polynomial \(f(x)\) of degree \(< d\):

1. Split: \(f(x) = f_{\text{even}}(x^2) + x \cdot f_{\text{odd}}(x^2)\)

2. Fold: \(f'(x) = f_{\text{even}}(x) + \alpha \cdot f_{\text{odd}}(x)\) with random \(\alpha\)

3. Recurse: \(f'\) has degree \(< d/2\)

4. Repeat until degree is small enough to send explicitly

Step 4: Merkle Commitment

Commit to polynomial evaluations using Merkle tree:

\[\text{root} = \text{MerkleCommit}(f(\omega^0), f(\omega^1), \ldots, f(\omega^{n-1}))\]

Proof Composition

\[\pi_{\text{STARK}} = (\text{trace\_root}, \text{FRI\_layers}, \text{queries})\]

Proof size: \(O(\log^2 n)\) due to Merkle authentication paths

Verification: Query random positions and verify consistency

4. Bulletproofs: Range Proofs

Problem Statement

Prove \(v \in [0, 2^n - 1]\) without revealing \(v\).

Inner Product Argument

Core building block: prove knowledge of vectors \(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{Z}_q^n\) such that:

\[c = \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b} \rangle = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i b_i\]

and commitments satisfy:

\[P = \mathbf{g}^{\mathbf{a}} \cdot \mathbf{h}^{\mathbf{b}} \cdot u^c\]

where \(\mathbf{g}, \mathbf{h}\) are generator vectors, \(u\) is another generator.

Range Proof Construction

Step 1: Express \(v\) in binary: \(v = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} v_i 2^i\) where \(v_i \in \{0,1\}\)

Step 2: Prove each \(v_i(v_i - 1) = 0\) using Pedersen commitments

Step 3: Aggregate proofs using inner product argument

Result: Logarithmic proof size \(O(\log n)\), much smaller than \(O(n)\) naive approach

5. Polynomial Commitment Schemes

KZG Commitments (Kate-Zaverucha-Goldberg)

Setup: Trusted setup generates powers of secret \(\tau\):

\[\text{SRS} = (g, g^{\tau}, g^{\tau^2}, \ldots, g^{\tau^d})\]

Commit: For polynomial \(f(x) = \sum_{i=0}^d a_i x^i\):

\[C = g^{f(\tau)} = \prod_{i=0}^d (g^{\tau^i})^{a_i}\]

Evaluation Proof: Prove \(f(z) = y\) for some \(z\):

Compute quotient polynomial: \(q(x) = \frac{f(x) - y}{x - z}\)

Proof: \(\pi = g^{q(\tau)}\)

Verify: Pairing check:

\[e(\pi, g^{\tau - z}) = e(C \cdot g^{-y}, g)\]

This verifies that \(q(\tau)(\tau - z) = f(\tau) - y\), hence \(f(z) = y\).

Properties

• Constant-size commitments and proofs: Single group element

• Efficient verification: Two pairings

• Batching: Can prove multiple evaluations efficiently

🔐 Cryptographic Assumptions and Security Reductions

1. Discrete Logarithm Problem (DLP)

Problem: Given cyclic group \(\mathbb{G}\) of order \(q\), generator \(g\), and \(y = g^x\), find \(x \in \mathbb{Z}_q\).

DLP Assumption: For any probabilistic polynomial-time (PPT) algorithm \(\mathcal{A}\):

\[\Pr[x \leftarrow \mathbb{Z}_q, y = g^x : \mathcal{A}(g, y) = x] \leq \text{negl}(\lambda)\]

Used in: Schnorr protocol, ElGamal, DSA

Best known attacks: Generic algorithms (Baby-step Giant-step, Pollard's rho) require \(O(\sqrt{q})\) group operations. For 256-bit groups, this is infeasible.

2. Computational Diffie-Hellman (CDH)

Problem: Given \(g^a, g^b\), compute \(g^{ab}\) without knowing \(a\) or \(b\).

CDH Assumption:

\[\Pr[a,b \leftarrow \mathbb{Z}_q : \mathcal{A}(g, g^a, g^b) = g^{ab}] \leq \text{negl}(\lambda)\]

Note: If DLP is hard, then CDH is hard. But CDH could be hard even if DLP has some weakness.

3. Decisional Diffie-Hellman (DDH)

Problem: Distinguish \((g^a, g^b, g^{ab})\) from \((g^a, g^b, g^c)\) where \(c\) is random.

DDH Assumption: For any PPT distinguisher \(\mathcal{D}\):

\[\left|\Pr[\mathcal{D}(g, g^a, g^b, g^{ab}) = 1] - \Pr[\mathcal{D}(g, g^a, g^b, g^c) = 1]\right| \leq \text{negl}(\lambda)\]

Hierarchy: DLP hard \(\Rightarrow\) CDH hard \(\Rightarrow\) DDH hard

Used in: ElGamal encryption, Pedersen commitments (perfect hiding)

4. Knowledge of Exponent Assumption (KEA)

Used in zk-SNARK security proofs.

KEA: If algorithm \(\mathcal{A}\) outputs \((C, C')\) such that \(C' = C^a\) where \(a\) is secret, then \(\mathcal{A}\) must "know" the exponent.

Formally, there exists extractor \(\mathcal{E}\) such that:

\[\Pr[\mathcal{A}(g, g^a) = (C, C') \wedge C' = C^a : \mathcal{E}(g, g^a; r_A) = x \text{ where } C = g^x] \geq 1 - \text{negl}(\lambda)\]

where \(r_A\) is \(\mathcal{A}\)'s random coins.

5. Bilinear Pairings and q-SDH

Bilinear pairing: \(e: \mathbb{G}_1 \times \mathbb{G}_2 \rightarrow \mathbb{G}_T\) such that:

• Bilinearity: \(e(g^a, h^b) = e(g, h)^{ab}\)

• Non-degeneracy: \(e(g, h) \neq 1_{\mathbb{G}_T}\) for generators \(g, h\)

• Efficiency: \(e\) is efficiently computable

q-Strong Diffie-Hellman (q-SDH) Assumption:

Given \((g, g^x, g^{x^2}, \ldots, g^{x^q})\), hard to compute \((c, g^{1/(x+c)})\) for any \(c \in \mathbb{Z}_p\).

Used in: BLS signatures, KZG polynomial commitments, Groth16

6. Collision-Resistant Hash Functions

Definition: Hash function \(H: \{0,1\}^* \rightarrow \{0,1\}^\lambda\) is collision-resistant if:

\[\Pr[\mathcal{A}() = (m, m') : m \neq m' \wedge H(m) = H(m')] \leq \text{negl}(\lambda)\]

Used in: Merkle trees, Fiat-Shamir transform, zk-STARKs

Examples: SHA-256, SHA-3, BLAKE2

Security Reduction Example: Schnorr Protocol Soundness

Theorem: If discrete logarithm is hard, then Schnorr protocol is sound (knowledge-sound).

Proof Sketch: Suppose cheating prover \(\mathcal{P}^*\) can produce valid proofs with non-negligible probability \(\epsilon\). We construct DLP solver \(\mathcal{B}\) using \(\mathcal{P}^*\):

Algorithm \(\mathcal{B}(g, y)\): (Goal: find \(x\) where \(y = g^x\))

1. Run \(\mathcal{P}^*(g, y)\) to get commitment \(t\)

2. Choose random challenge \(c_1\), get response \(s_1\)

3. Rewind \(\mathcal{P}^*\) to same point, choose different \(c_2 \neq c_1\)

4. Get second response \(s_2\)

5. If both verify, extract: \(x = (s_1 - s_2)(c_1 - c_2)^{-1} \bmod q\)

6. Return \(x\)

Analysis: By forking lemma (Pointcheval-Stern), if \(\mathcal{P}^*\) succeeds with probability \(\epsilon\), then with probability \(\approx \epsilon^2\), \(\mathcal{B}\) obtains two valid transcripts and extracts \(x\).

If \(\epsilon\) is non-negligible, then \(\epsilon^2\) is non-negligible, contradicting DLP hardness. Therefore, no such \(\mathcal{P}^*\) exists. □

Concrete Security vs. Asymptotic Security

Asymptotic: Security holds for sufficiently large \(\lambda\), expressed as \(\text{negl}(\lambda)\)

Concrete: Actual security level for specific parameters

Example: For \(\lambda = 128\)-bit security:

• Discrete log: Use groups of size \(\geq 2^{256}\) (256-bit curve)

• Hash function: Output length \(\geq 256\) bits (SHA-256)

• Soundness error: \(\leq 2^{-128}\)

This means attacker needs \(\approx 2^{128}\) operations to break security, infeasible with current/near-future technology.
